# Socratic questions

The teaching note's questions are the instrument. A bad question
turns the discussion into a survey of opinions; a good question forces
the student to commit, defend, and revise.

## Five properties of a good question

1. **Supported by evidence and declared preparation.** Questions may use facts from the case and theory from specified prerequisites or assigned readings. If a framework is needed, make it available in the preparation and explain its application in the teaching note; do not add theory to the student case solely to support a question.
2. **Elicit reasoning.** Use open analytical questions; a factual or yes/no check may help when followed by a request for evidence or explanation.
3. **Sequenced concrete → analytical → judgment.** Block 1 starts with observation. Block 2 moves to analysis. Block 3 demands a position. Block 4 invites transfer.
4. **Specific, not generic.** "What would you do?" is generic. "Would you cut the bottom 10% of premium borrowers knowing that segment drives 40% of new originations?" is specific.
5. **No answer key in the question.** Worked analysis and plausible responses belong in the instructor-facing teaching note, not in the wording of the student question.

## Question patterns

### Cold open (Block 1)

- "What did you notice first when you read the case?"
- "What would you want to know before making the decision?"
- "Where in the case does the protagonist first signal discomfort?"

### Dilemma + data (Block 2)

- "What is the protagonist giving up if they choose option A?"
- "Which exhibit answers which question the protagonist is asking?"
- "What evidence contradicts the protagonist's preferred outcome?"
- "Where is the most fragile number in the case?"

### Decision moment (Block 3)

- "What would you do?"
- "What would the protagonist's harshest critic say?"
- "What would the protagonist say in their defense?"
- "What does the protagonist lose by not deciding?"

### Wrap-up + transfer (Block 4)

- "Where have you seen this pattern before?"
- "What does the case predict for the next quarter? For next year?"
- "If the protagonist had a board, what would they have done differently?"
- "What does the case teach that no theory teaches on its own?"

## What NOT to do

- Don't lead: "Don't you think the protagonist was wrong to...?" — destroys the instrument.
- Don't summarize: "So the protagonist faced X and chose Y. What do you think?" — the case already said that.
- Don't lecture: a long instructor monologue between questions drains the room.
- Don't anchor: if the first student gives the answer you want, don't let the rest just agree.

## Cold call vs open call

- **Cold call**: pick a specific student to answer. Use according to the instructor’s approach, cohort, and participation goals; it can test comprehension.
- **Open call**: invite volunteers. Default for most questions.

A 20-minute block might use one cold call and two or three open calls; adapt to the discussion rather than enforce a quota.

## How many questions per block

Aim for 4–6 pre-written questions per block. Pick 2–3 live based on class
energy. The remaining questions are backups if the discussion stalls.
