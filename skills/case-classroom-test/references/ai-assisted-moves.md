# AI-assisted student moves

A catalogue of moves students are likely to make when they have access
to AI assistants during a case discussion. The skill flags the case
sections most vulnerable and suggests counter-questions that block
the shortcut without becoming obvious.

## Categories of moves

### C1. Spreadsheet shortcut

- **Description**: the student runs a spreadsheet on the case's financial data and produces a numerical answer.
- **Risk**: the discussion becomes about the spreadsheet's result, not about the decision.
- **Counter-question pattern**: "Even if the spreadsheet says X, what non-financial considerations does the protagonist face?"
- **Example**: a case asking "what's the IRR?" — counter-question "should the IRR threshold be the same for a strategic versus financial investment?"

### C2. Summarization shortcut

- **Description**: the student asks AI to summarize the case or to extract the decision.
- **Risk**: students with no real engagement can produce a fluent summary.
- **Counter-question pattern**: "If the case is summarized in three sentences, what does the protagonist lose that the summary can't capture?"
- **Example**: a long case that compresses cleanly — counter-question "what would the protagonist say is missing from the summary?"

### C3. Position shortcut

- **Description**: the student asks AI to take a position ("what should the protagonist do?").
- **Risk**: students parrot AI's position without engaging.
- **Counter-question pattern**: "If you disagree with AI's recommendation, what is your strongest counter-argument and what evidence from the case supports it?"
- **Example**: a case with a clear "right answer" — counter-question forces engagement with the case evidence.

### C4. Counter-argument shortcut

- **Description**: the student asks AI to produce the strongest counter-argument and recites it.
- **Risk**: the discussion becomes a contest of who's better at prompting.
- **Counter-question pattern**: "If you were the protagonist's harshest critic, what would you say that AI is not saying?"
- **Example**: a case where AI's standard counter-arguments are predictable — counter-question forces the student beyond the model's standard repertoire.

### C5. Framework shortcut

- **Description**: the student asks AI to apply a named framework (Porter, SWOT, etc.) and recites.
- **Risk**: the discussion becomes about the framework, not the decision.
- **Counter-question pattern**: "Apply a framework the protagonist has not heard of — what would they conclude?"
- **Example**: a case that maps cleanly onto Porter's Five Forces — counter-question forces engagement with the protagonist's actual constraints.

## Where to put AI moves in the classroom/report.md report

```markdown
## AI-assisted moves catalogue

| Move | Target section | Counter-question |
|---|---|---|
| C1 spreadsheet | Block 2 question on IRR | "Even if IRR is 14%, what non-financial considerations does the protagonist face?" |
| C2 summarize | Part A context | "What would the protagonist say is missing from a 3-sentence summary?" |
| C3 position | Part C decision | "If you disagree with AI's recommendation, what is your strongest counter-argument?" |
```

The catalogue is a living document. Refresh after every pilot.

## Anti-patterns

- **Do not add a trap.** Students see through traps; the case loses credibility.
- **Do not forbid AI.** The audit is on the discussion, not the tool. Students who use AI should still produce the counter-question in their own words.
- **Do not pretend AI doesn't exist.** Acknowledge it as a class resource; ask students to engage with it critically.
